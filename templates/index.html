A lab result is a number. A decision needs a reason.

Most soil tools either stop at the numbers or hide the reasoning. It does the agronomist's job in the open: it turns each reading into a degree of "good" or "poor" for the crop you chose, applies the rules a soil scientist would, and tells you which factor is holding the plot back.

1 of 4

Read the soil

pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, organic carbon, salinity, moisture and texture. From a lab sheet, a test kit, or your hands.

2 of 4

Blur the edges

A pH of 5.8 is not simply "acidic". It is mostly fine and a little sour. Fuzzy membership keeps that honesty instead of forcing a label.

3 of 4

Apply the rules

A short rule base per crop, generated from published crop requirements and the FAO principle that the weakest factor sets the ceiling.

4 of 4

Give a graded answer

A score from 0 to 100, an FAO class from S1 to N, the limiting factors in order, and what to do about each.

Six crops that feed Nigerian markets

Each crop carries its own requirements. Onion will not forgive salt that tomato shrugs off; cabbage wants the clay loam that onion hates.
